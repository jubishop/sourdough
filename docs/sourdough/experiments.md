# Sourdough Future Experiment #4 — Higher Hydration Using Bassinage

**Updated:** November 9, 2026  
**Status:** Existing unprioritized backlog experiment; **not** an instruction to change the current recipe or upcoming bake.  
**Technique name:** **Bassinage**, also called **double hydration** — reserving some additional water until after the flour-and-water autolyse.

## Question

How will our 100%-whole-wheat loaf behave if we raise hydration **without changing the original autolyse mixture**, adding the extra water only when levain and salt are incorporated?

## Exact proposed procedure

1. **Same autolyse as the current formula:** combine **500 g whole-wheat flour + 400 g water** and rest for the current baseline **90 minutes**. **Do not add the experimental water at this stage.**
2. At the usual post-autolyse step, add **100 g of 100%-hydration levain** and **12 g of salt**. **Add the experimental extra water at this time** (bassinage), preferably a little at a time, mixing thoroughly until absorbed. Don't force more water into dough if it cannot take it well; record actual quantity incorporated.
3. Proceed with unchanged folds, bulk fermentation, shaping, cold proof, scoring and baking. Count bulk from levain incorporation as usual.

## Suggested controlled comparison

| Trial | Whole-wheat flour in initial autolyse | Water in initial autolyse | Extra water via bassinage, with levain/salt | Levain (100% hydration) | Salt | Overall hydration |
| --- | ---: | ---: | ---: | ---: | ---: | ---: |
| Current control | 500 g | 400 g | **0 g** | 100 g | 12 g | **450/550 = 81.8%** |
| First higher-hydration trial | 500 g | 400 g | **20 g** | 100 g | 12 g | **470/550 = 85.5%** |

The levain contributes **50 g flour and 50 g water** to both trials; total flour remains **550 g**. The extra 20 g is a **possible first trial**, not a selected next-bake change. One could try +10 g first or another amount after reviewing results. Do not simultaneously change the main flour or levain amount.

## Hold constant / observations

- Keep the flour and its brand, autolyse duration, levain quantity/maturity, salt amount, fold schedule, proof-box setting, bulk stop point, shaping, cold proof, Dutch oven setup and baking profile unchanged between bakes. Current working box choice is **fixed 76°F** and bulk endpoint **1 US quart at the glass edge**, subject to any later user decisions.
- Record ease of adding/absorbing the extra water, dough feel during folds, extensibility versus stickiness, shape retention when turning out, loaf height/spread, oven spring, crumb openness, base compression and gumminess.
- Separate the plausible benefit of slightly higher hydration from risks of slack dough and wetter crumb. The outcome is uncertain; this is a hypothesis, not a prediction of improvement.
- Take comparable exterior and crumb photographs, and confirm a favourable result on a separate bake before proposing any baseline update.

**Relationship to backlog:** Clarifies the existing hydration experiment **#4**. It does **not** create an additional experiment number and does not change the next bake.

# Sourdough Future Experiment #5 — Cold-Proof Duration

**Detailed plan added:** November 9, 2026  
**Status:** Proposed future, controlled experiment. Priority **#9** in the November 9 first-pass experiment ranking; user may rearrange it later. **Do not change the current loaf, baseline recipe, or published website.**  
**Question:** For our 100% whole-wheat sourdough, does a shorter refrigerator proof improve crumb openness, structural integrity and **vertical oven spring**, without worsening the finished crumb's damp/gummy mouthfeel?

## Background and interpretation

- From the whole-wheat sourdough recipes and baker discussions reviewed November 9, **roughly 8–16 hours** is a commonly described overnight window, and **12–14 hours is conventional**, not inherently excessive. Some bakers prefer longer refrigerated proofs for taste or convenience; others report better spring with shorter proofing or same-day baking. There is **no established universal optimum** for this recipe.
- The loaf scheduled for **November 10, 2026** is expected to receive approximately **13 hours** of cold proof (overnight November 9–10). This is a **planned estimate, not a verified finished bake**, and other variables have also been adjusted around this loaf. Treat its outcome as a **reference observation**, not proof of how changing cold proof alone affects the result.
- Fermentation continues while a newly refrigerated loaf cools. Thus hours in the refrigerator are not identical to hours at a steady cold dough temperature. **The state of the dough at the end of bulk and the actual refrigerator temperature matter as much as clock time.**
- Refrigerating a covered loaf longer does **not** directly dry the crumb. Changes to gumminess or perceived dryness, if observed, would more plausibly come from differences in fermentation, acidity, starch/gluten structure, or gas retention. Do not try to compensate by extending uncovered oven baking; earlier oven-drying experiments did not solve this problem.

## Proposed comparison: one variable at a time

| Trial | Refrigerator proof | Interpretation |
| --- | ---: | --- |
| **Reference / longer** | **13 hours** | Typical overnight range; corresponds to the estimated Nov 9–10 bake, but recreate under matched conditions for a controlled A/B |
| **Shorter trial** | **8 hours** | Large enough reduction to plausibly reveal differences in spring, crumb distribution and acidity |

The useful first formal test is **8 vs. 13 hours**, ideally in separate bakes with otherwise identical conditions. If 8 hours is impractical, a 10-hour intermediate trial is acceptable for scheduling, but it is not part of the primary A/B. Confirm actual fridge-in and oven-loading times; do not mistake an intended duration for a measured one. A subsequent longer/shorter range can be explored only if these results justify it.

## Hold constant across the formal comparison

- **Dough formula:** 500 g King Arthur stone-ground 100% whole-wheat flour + 400 g water + 100 g 100%-hydration whole-wheat levain + 12 g salt; 550 g total flour / 450 g total water, ~81.8% total hydration.
- **Autolyse:** 90 min. **Fold schedule:** current test uses two fold sessions at T+30 and T+60 after levain/salt incorporation; by the time #5 is tested, use whichever fold schedule has become the *established* reference, unchanged across both trials.
- **Bulk fermentation:** current working setting is **72°F** to **1.0 L at the glass edge** (as of Nov 9). Use the same proven setting and endpoint in both formal trials, log elapsed bulk time, dough dome and actual temperature. Do not force the dough to finish bulk at an identical clock time.
- Same flour lot if possible, levain amount/hydration/**maturity at mixing**, mixing method, shaping and banneton, no pre-shaping chill, Dutch-oven covered/uncovered temperatures and times, liners, scoring, and cooling before slicing.
- Same refrigerator and shelf placement, ideally with a measured **~37–39°F** interior temperature if that is what the fridge actually maintains; this is a **suggested reference range, not an observed reading**. Cover loaves consistently to avoid surface drying. Record refrigerator setting/actual temperature and, if possible, dough temperature at refrigeration and loading.
- **Scheduling caveat:** The user is considering moving from a levain built overnight (~70–71°F, can pass peak/collapse after ~12 hours) to a morning levain accelerated in an ~80°F proofing box to fit a shorter cold proof before bedtime. This may be a practical improvement, but changing **levain build temperature, feeding ratio or maturity simultaneously with cold-proof duration would confound the experiment**. Establish a comparable ripe levain at mixing for the 8-hour and 13-hour trials, or explicitly log them as different-process observations rather than claiming a clean duration comparison. The proposed daytime build is **not yet an adopted method**.

## Record and evaluate

- Time shaped dough enters refrigerator, time loaded into oven and **actual elapsed cold-proof hours**; refrigerator temperature, dough shape before/after cold proof, and evidence of over- or underproofing.
- Time/temperature/dough shape at the 1.0 L bulk endpoint and levain maturity when mixed.
- Loaf **maximum height, width/footprint, side spread, vertical spring, score opening and blowouts**; standardized exterior photos.
- **Crumb moisture/tackiness after comparable complete cooling**; proportion and distribution of air pockets, bottom compression, dense bands and unwanted large shaping voids; standardized central cross-section photo.
- Flavour/acidity and whether the shorter schedule gives up too much development for any improvement in spring.

## Decision rule

If 8 hours reproducibly provides **less gummy-feeling crumb**, more even openness or greater **vertical spring** without other meaningful tradeoffs, consider making it the standard. If 13 hours performs as well or better, retain the more convenient overnight proof. A single bake cannot prove the cold-proof duration caused a change, especially if levain preparation, folding, or bulk endpoint also differs. **Nothing graduates to Baseline without explicit user approval.**

**Related records:** `/Sourdough/Sourdough-Goals-and-Experiment-Priorities.md` and `/Sourdough/Sourdough-Bake-Journal.md`.

# Sourdough Future Experiment #10 — No Deliberate Shaping

**Added:** November 6, 2026
**Status:** Unprioritized future trial; not necessarily the very next bake.
**Goal:** See whether eliminating the shaping phase prevents the recurring underside “belly button” while preserving acceptable loaf height and crumb.

## Why try it?
- The 100%-whole-wheat dough already feels **quite firm** after normal bulk fermentation and rounds of folds.
- The underside has repeatedly gathered into a knot or cavern after shaping.
- On November 6, even **without the 15-minute pre-shaping refrigeration or any countertop tightening pulls**, the gathered cavity was especially large (photo: `/Sourdough/2026-11-06-belly-button-preproof.jpeg`). The chill cannot be the sole cause.
- On November 6, user instead shaped by gently cupping the dough with their hands, and a major cavity still appeared. User wants to test whether *all intentional shaping*, including hand cupping, contributes more harm than benefit for this particular dough.

## Controlled test
1. Prepare the same 100%-whole-wheat formula and use the usual autolyse, levain, salt, folding schedule, and a **fixed, explicitly logged bulk-fermentation endpoint** selected for the shaping comparison: either Baseline **1.0 L** or the experimental **1 US quart (~946 mL)**. Do not switch endpoints in the same shaping-method comparison. Record actuals and note which recipe track supplied them.
2. **No pre-shaping refrigeration**, matching the November 6 handling.
3. At the end of bulk, release the dough gently from the container and transfer it to the prepared, rice-floured banneton, ideally preserving its smooth existing surface against the basket so it will be exposed on turning out to bake. Use a scraper or minimal flour only if needed to release it.
4. **No preshape, rolling or envelope folds, pulling toward yourself on the counter, tightening, deliberate seam-making, or seam-pinching.** Avoid adding a new variable by attempting to compensate with extra folds.
5. Keep cold-proof duration/temperature, scoring, Dutch-oven preheat, covered/uncovered bake temperatures and durations, and **the two perforated silicone liners** as close as possible to the comparison bake. Do not change hydration or the bulk endpoint in the same test.
6. Photograph the dough as it goes into the banneton, after the cold proof when turned out, and after baking; photograph the cooled crumb.

## Measurements to compare
- Presence/size of underside belly button or folded/cavitated seam.
- Dough release, surface integrity, shape in the basket, and how much it spreads after turn-out.
- Loaf height, oven spring, score expansion, and any side blowouts.
- Bottom crumb compression, hole size/evenness, gumminess after full cooling, crust appearance.
- Handling effort and reproducibility.

## How to read results
- **No cavity, similar spring:** even hand cupping may be unnecessary, though this single test cannot establish which earlier handling motion causes the defect.
- **No cavity, but flatter loaf:** shaping does provide useful surface tension; seek a gentler alternative rather than abandoning it permanently.
- **Cavity still present:** look for the fold/cavity occurring during container release, bulk folding, or transfer, not just countertop pulls.
- A single loaf is suggestive but not definitive; do not conclude causation from appearance alone.

**Experiment queue:** This is item #10, alongside the existing nine unprioritized experiments and the separate alternative-shaping family (#11); it does **not** supersede their order or set the next bake plan. **No pre-shaping fridge** is now a baseline standard, not a variable to keep retesting unless new evidence warrants it.

# Sourdough Future Experiment #11 — Alternative Shaping Techniques

**Added:** November 6, 2026
**Status:** Unprioritized future experiment family; **each method is a separate bake**, not a combination.
**Question:** Can we create enough structure for a well-risen oval 100% whole-wheat loaf without gathering a deep underside belly-button cavity?

## Why this matters
- The 100%-whole-wheat dough already feels quite firm after the usual bulk and fold rounds.
- Prior countertop tightening pulls created an underside knot. On November 6, the dough produced an especially large knot **even though no countertop pulls were used**—it was shaped only by cupping with the hands.
- The pre-shape fridge has now been eliminated from the *baseline standard*, because dough releases from its container well without it.
- **Bulk target remains undecided:** the live website reportedly has separate Baseline and Experiment recipes, and the *Experiment* ends bulk at **1 US quart (~946 mL)** rather than the previous **1.0 L**. Keep fermentation fixed while comparing shaping trials; never treat the 1-qt endpoint as proven by itself.

## Individual shaping-method trials

### A. Broad envelope folds; no counter dragging
Previously discussed November 5. Turn out dough gently and spread only as needed into a thick rounded rectangle/square. Fold the left, right, top, and bottom portions across as **broad, flat overlapping layers**, not a tight central pinch or little package. Transfer seam-up into the floured oval banneton. **Do not flip the dough and drag it around the countertop.** Compare underside fold and loaf spring.

### B. Gentle fold-and-roll batard
Turn out into a thick rectangle, fold sides inward with minimal degassing, then roll into an elongated oval log suitable for the user's oval banneton, creating modest surface tension through the roll rather than by cupping/dragging the entire ball. Seal only the longitudinal seam **lightly**, not by pushing all the dough into a center point. Transfer seam-up into banneton. Compare seam geometry, shape, and oven spring.

### C. Minimal counter tightening, only if worth testing later
The historically discussed comparison: use at most **two short, very gentle pulls** (versus the older roughly four stronger pulls), stopping when the outside is barely taut. Do not flip the dough halfway through the shaping process to inspect—this was previously rejected because it would disturb tension. Photograph underside as soon as it goes seam-up into the banneton. This is a trial, not a default recommendation; hand-cupping without pulling also failed to prevent the knot.

### D. No deliberate shaping (tracked separately as Experiment #10)
Move directly from bulk container to banneton with no preshape, pinching, folding, rolling, hand cupping, or dragging. Reference `/Sourdough/Experiment-10-No-Shaping.md`. This is the cleanest counterpoint to methods A–C.

## Controls and evaluation
- Continue **100% whole wheat**, consistent flour/hydration/levain/salt and usual bulk folds, **no pre-shaping refrigeration**, the same chosen bulk target, cold proof, score, and bake profile, including the two perforated silicone liners plus circular King Arthur silicone liner specified in the current recipes.
- Keep only one shaping method per loaf. Note degree of tension, handling time, seam appearance, and any collapse on turn-out.
- Photograph underside immediately after transfer, then baked top/side, then crumb after full cooling.
- Compare **belly-button size/inner fold**, bottom compression, crumb uniformity, spread, loaf height, oven spring, side tearing, and ease of handling.
- A technique giving no underside cavity but a noticeably flatter loaf is a tradeoff, not an automatic win.
- Hold judgments until bake outcomes are available and avoid prematurely migrating any shaping method to Baseline.

# Sourdough Future Experiment #12 — Levain Quantity

**Added:** November 9, 2026  
**Status:** Unprioritized future experiment, not an instruction to change the current recipe or next bake.  
**Question:** What happens to our 100% whole-wheat sourdough if we use **less or more than our current 100 g of levain**?

## Experimental rationale

The existing working formula uses **100 g of 100%-hydration whole-wheat levain**. The user wants to explore smaller and larger inoculations. Less levain will generally mean a slower rise; more levain generally means a faster rise. Levain also carries mature fermented flour and acidity, so changing its amount **may** alter dough strength, flavor, and the final crumb. The final outcome is not predetermined; in particular, more levain does **not** always guarantee a more sour loaf, because fermentation time changes as well.

## Proposed three-bake comparison

Hold **total flour 550 g, total water 450 g, salt 12 g, and nominal overall hydration ~81.8%** constant. All levain is assumed to be at **100% hydration**. Alter the separately added flour and water to compensate exactly for the flour and water already in the levain:

| Trial | Levain | Main flour (100% whole wheat) | Main water | Salt | Flour from levain / total flour |
| --- | ---: | ---: | ---: | ---: | ---: |
| Less | **50 g** | 525 g | 425 g | 12 g | 25/550 = 4.5% |
| Control (current) | **100 g** | 500 g | 400 g | 12 g | 50/550 = 9.1% |
| More | **150 g** | 475 g | 375 g | 12 g | 75/550 = 13.6% |

Total dough mass remains 1,012 g, excluding handling water. The proposed 50 and 150 g levels are **starting points for a future test**, not agreed recipe changes. Each trial should be an independent bake with only levain amount changed.

## Keep consistent and document

- Use the same flour, levain hydration and **similar levain maturity at mixing**. Track levain peak/age, since maturity can change acidity and performance.
- Keep autolyse, folding, shaping, 100% whole-wheat flour, salt, refrigerator/cold-proof conditions, baking temperatures, and successful liner setup unchanged across the trials.
- Use the chosen **fixed 76°F proofing-box setting**, unless it has been deliberately updated before these future tests. The current working bulk target is **1 US quart (~946 mL at the glass edge)**. Keep it the same but log dough dome/height so differences in appearance do not get mistaken for equivalent actual fermentation.
- Start the bulk clock when levain is incorporated. Record time to reach the bulk mark, actual dough temperature, ease of folding/shaping, strength/spread, acidity/sourness, oven spring, crumb openness, base compression, and gumminess.
- Photograph exterior and cooled crumb and compare to the 100 g control. Repeat promising results before calling them improvements.

**Decision rule:** Identify whether slower/faster fermentation and any changes in crumb, handling, or flavor are worth adopting. Do not migrate to Baseline without the user's explicit approval. This experiment remains **unprioritized**.

# Sourdough Future Experiment #13 — Salt Quantity

**Added:** November 9, 2026  
**Status:** Unprioritized future experiment; do not change the live recipe or next bake without explicit approval.  
**Question:** What changes in taste, fermentation, dough handling, and crumb when we use less or more than the current **12 g salt** in a 100% whole-wheat loaf?

## Why this is interesting

Salt does more than season bread. It generally moderates yeast fermentation, affects gluten behavior and dough elasticity, and can make dough feel more cohesive. In 100% whole-wheat dough, its effect may interact with bran, the dough's existing firmness, and the fermentation endpoint. Salt level also changes perceived sourness and overall flavor balance. The direction and magnitude of finished-loaf changes should be measured rather than assumed.

## Proposed controlled comparison — separate bakes

Keep **total flour 550 g**, **total water 450 g** (~81.8% hydration), and **100 g of 100%-hydration whole-wheat levain** unchanged. The main formula remains **500 g whole-wheat flour + 400 g water + 100 g levain**, with only salt quantity varied.

| Trial | Salt | Baker's % of total flour | Purpose |
| --- | ---: | ---: | --- |
| Less | **10 g** | **1.82%** | Test if less salt improves dough extensibility / changes fermentation and flavor |
| Current control | **12 g** | **2.18%** | Established recipe quantity, *not* a demonstrated optimum |
| More | **14 g** | **2.55%** | Test if more salt changes structure, pace of rise, and perceived taste |

These are **suggested trial quantities**, not agreed changes to the baseline. They provide a meaningful, relatively modest range on either side of the existing formula. Each quantity gets its own independent loaf. Salt changes the total dough mass slightly (±2 g), but keep flour and water constant to isolate the salt concentration as well as possible.

## Controls

- Use the same flour, levain quantity/maturity, hydration, mixing technique, and the same autolyse duration (**90 minutes** unless the chosen baseline changes beforehand); add salt with the levain **after** the flour-and-water autolyse, at the same stage each time.
- Keep the folding schedule, fixed proofing-box setting (currently **76°F**), user-approved **1 US quart (~946 mL) at-glass bulk endpoint**, shaping, cold-proof time/temperature, scoring, Dutch oven / liners, and baking schedule the same across trials unless a later baseline is chosen beforehand.
- Because salt can change fermentation speed, record **time from levain/salt incorporation to reaching the bulk mark**, the dough temperature and dome, and whether readiness looks comparable at that mark. Do not force identical bulk times at the expense of comparing equivalent fermentation endpoints.
- Record resistance/extensibility during folds, shaping tension, stickiness/spread, oven spring, score opening, crumb structure (especially base compression), gumminess after full cooling, crust, and taste.
- For taste, ideally compare similar-thickness slices cooled for the same duration. Consider a blind comparison, noting that added salt can make a loaf seem more flavorful even without changes in fermentation acidity.

## Decision

Compare the 10 g and 14 g loaves against the 12 g control. Prefer the level that gives the best combination of taste, fermentation predictability, dough handling, oven spring, and crumb quality. Repeat any promising improvement before making it the Baseline recipe, and obtain user approval before changing the published formula.

**Backlog only:** No date or order assigned; this does not supersede the currently planned next-bake folding experiment.
